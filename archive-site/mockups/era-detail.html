A historian’s overview

12 min read

The two decades between 1950 and 1970 compressed centuries of psychoactive encounter into a single generation. What began as a quiet pharmacological curiosity — Albert Hofmann’s LSD-25, synthesized in 1938 but only re-discovered in 1943 — arrived in postwar America as a research tool with no settled meaning. Was it a model psychosis? An adjunct to psychotherapy? A weapon? By 1970 the U.S. Congress would answer by classifying it, alongside psilocybin, mescaline, and DMT, as a Schedule I substance with “no currently accepted medical use.”

“The psychedelic era did not arrive; it was assembled, piece by piece, out of pharmacology, anthropology, Cold War paranoia, and a generation looking for the doors of perception to open.” — from the editor’s introduction

The archive’s holdings for this period reflect that assembly. Sandoz Laboratories distributed thousands of LSD ampules to psychiatrists worldwide, generating a vast clinical literature now scattered across journals, hospital archives, and personal correspondence. R. Gordon Wasson’s 1957 Life photograph of a Mazatec ceremony opened the floodgates of ethnographic interest. The CIA’s MKULTRA program — documented here through declassified memoranda — ran in parallel, funding much of the same research under cover names.

By the mid-1960s the laboratory had spilled into the street. Underground chemists, countercultural presses, and a thriving black market produced the cultural rupture that would, in turn, produce prohibition. The sources collected here trace that arc not as inevitability but as contingency — a sequence of choices, encounters, and accidents.
